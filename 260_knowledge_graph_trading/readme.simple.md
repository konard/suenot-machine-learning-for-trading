# Knowledge Graph Trading — Explained Simply

## What is a Knowledge Graph?

Imagine a giant map of how things are connected. Think of a social media app where you can see who is friends with whom. A knowledge graph is like that, but for the business world — it shows which companies work together, compete against each other, or share the same customers.

For example: Apple buys chips from TSMC. TSMC also sells to NVIDIA. If TSMC has a problem, both Apple and NVIDIA could be affected. A knowledge graph makes these hidden connections visible.

## How Does It Work? The Web of Relationships

Picture a spider web. Each point where threads meet is a company, a person, or a product. Each thread connecting two points is a relationship — like "supplies parts to" or "competes with."

A computer stores these as simple facts, like filling in a sentence: **(Company A) —[relationship]→ (Company B)**

- *(Tesla) —[buys batteries from]→ (Panasonic)*
- *(Google) —[competes with]→ (Microsoft)*
- *(Warren Buffett) —[CEO of]→ (Berkshire Hathaway)*

## Turning the Map into Numbers

Computers cannot think about webs and maps directly. They need numbers. So we teach the computer to give every company and every relationship a set of numbers (called an "embedding") that captures its position in the web.

Think of it like GPS coordinates. Just as GPS tells you that a coffee shop and a bakery are close together on a real map, embeddings tell the computer that two companies are "close" in the business world — maybe because they are in the same industry or share many suppliers.

## Why This Helps with Trading

### The Domino Effect

Imagine a row of dominoes. If one falls, the next ones fall too. In business, the same thing happens:

1. A car factory announces record sales (good news!)
2. The steel company that supplies metal to the factory will probably do well too
3. The mining company that sells iron ore to the steel company might benefit as well

Without a knowledge graph, a trader might only notice the car factory's news. With a knowledge graph, the computer can trace the chain and find opportunities in the steel and mining companies *before* other traders notice.

### Finding Hidden Risks

Imagine you have three jars of cookies, each from a different bakery. You think your cookie supply is safe because you do not depend on just one bakery. But what if all three bakeries get their flour from the same mill? If the mill closes, all your cookies disappear!

Knowledge graphs help investors find these hidden "single points of failure" in their portfolios.

### Event Ripples

When a big event happens — like a new law or a product recall — the knowledge graph shows exactly which other companies will be affected, like ripples spreading across a pond when you throw a stone.

## How Computers Learn to Predict

1. **Build the web**: Collect facts about companies and their relationships
2. **Learn the map**: Turn the web into numbers (embeddings) that capture how close or far apart companies are
3. **Find patterns**: Notice that when Company A goes up, connected Company B often follows
4. **Make predictions**: When new information arrives, trace its path through the web to find trading opportunities

## Why This Matters

- **For traders**: Find profitable connections that others miss
- **For risk managers**: Spot hidden risks lurking in supply chains
- **For analysts**: Understand the full picture of how industries connect
- **For everyone**: Markets work better when participants understand how companies are truly linked

## Try It Yourself

1. Pick a company you know (like Apple or Tesla)
2. List five companies it does business with (suppliers, customers, partners)
3. For each of those, list their connections too
4. Draw the web on paper — you have just made a mini knowledge graph!
5. Now imagine: if one company in your web has a problem, which others would feel it?
6. That is exactly how knowledge graph trading works, but with thousands of companies and a computer doing the thinking
